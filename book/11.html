The Easy Way to Quit YouTube Addiction

"At Least It's Not Drugs"

Imagine someone who drinks a bottle of vodka every night says to you, "At least it's not heroin."

Are you impressed by their self-awareness? Do you nod and think, Good point — the existence of a worse substance does make the vodka harmless? Or do you recognize that they've just dodged the question entirely? The question was never "Is vodka the worst possible thing you could be doing?" The question was "Is this helping you?"

The "at least it's not drugs" defense is one of the Big Monster's favorite moves. It's the argument that sounds like perspective but functions as deflection. It lets you compare your habit to something dramatically worse, feel a small flush of relief — see, I'm not that bad — and then close the case without ever actually examining what your habit is doing to you.

And it works because the comparison is true. YouTube is not heroin. It's not alcohol. It's not gambling. You're not going to overdose on commentary videos. You're not going to lose your house betting on Shorts. The consequences are not dramatic. They're not visible. They don't make good intervention television. So when the Big Monster whispers at least it's not drugs, it's not lying. It's just answering a question you didn't ask.

The question you need to answer isn't "Is YouTube the worst thing?" It's "Is YouTube serving me?" And the existence of heroin has nothing to do with that.

If someone said, "I can't be drowning — the ocean is much deeper over there," you'd point out that drowning is drowning, regardless of what's happening in the deeper water. The depth elsewhere doesn't change the water in your lungs. The existence of worse addictions doesn't change what this one is doing to your time, your attention, your sleep, your presence, your life. The comparison is a distraction. The Big Monster wants you looking over there so you never look right here.

And right here, the harm is real. It's just quiet. That's what makes it so easy to dismiss. There's no hangover from a YouTube binge — just a vague flatness, a slightly emptier feeling than before you opened the app. There's no financial ruin — just hours that disappeared, one after another, that you'll never get back. There's no dramatic rock bottom — just a slow, steady erosion of the things you said mattered to you. Sleep that got shortened by "one more video." Conversations that got half-attended because the screen was still playing. Projects that never started because the watching always came first. A life that's been on pause, not because something terrible happened, but because something mildly interesting was always playing.

The harm is subtle. It's cumulative. It's a leak in the roof that drips one drop per hour — no flood, no collapse, just a slow, quiet dampness spreading through the walls. You could ignore it for years. You probably have. And the Big Monster has been there the whole time, pointing at hurricanes on the news, saying see? At least it's not that.

But slow damage is still damage. A leak you ignore doesn't stop leaking just because you've decided it's not a hurricane. It keeps dripping. The dampness spreads. The structure weakens. And one day you look up and realize the room you're standing in is not the room you meant to build your life in.

What if you applied a different standard? Not the standard of "is this the worst possible thing?" but the standard you'd use for someone you actually care about.

Imagine someone you love — a partner, a child, a close friend — came to you and said: I'm spending hours every day watching something I don't fully choose to watch. I'm losing sleep. I'm postponing things I care about. I feel guilty about it. I feel defensive when anyone brings it up. But at least it's not drugs.

Would you accept that? Would you say, "You're right, the fact that heroin exists means your obvious distress is nothing to worry about"? Or would you say something closer to: "I'm not asking whether it's the worst thing in the world. I'm asking whether it's helping you live the life you want to live."

You know which one you'd say. You know because you're not cruel, and you're not indifferent, and when someone you love is clearly struggling, you don't let them deflect with comparisons. You want them to be free — not because their cage is the worst cage, but because they're in a cage at all.

So why accept the deflection from yourself?

The Big Monster has trained you to compare outward so you never have to look inward. Outward, there's always someone worse off. There's always a more dramatic addiction, a more visible ruin, a more urgent cautionary tale. You can always find a comparison that makes your own situation look manageable by contrast. But inward — when you stop comparing and just look at what's actually happening — the question changes. It's no longer "Am I as bad as that person?" It's "Is this making my life better or worse?" And that question doesn't care about heroin. It doesn't care about gambling. It doesn't care what anyone else is doing. It only cares about you, and your hours, and your attention, and the gap between the life you're living and the life you could be living.

The only comparison that matters is the one between you with this compulsion and you without it. Not YouTube versus drugs. Not YouTube versus other people's worse habits. Just your life, evaluated honestly.

You don't need to be at rock bottom to want something better. You don't need to be in the worst cage to want out of yours. The fact that worse things exist does not make a harmful thing harmless. A broken leg is still broken even if someone else has a broken spine. The hours you lose, the sleep you miss, the attention you never fully own, the life you postpone — none of that becomes acceptable just because other people are dealing with worse.

The question was never "Is this the worst thing?" The question is "Is this helping you live the life you actually want?" And if the answer to that question is no — even quietly, even without drama, even if everything looks fine from the outside — then the comparison to worse things was never a defense. It was just a way of avoiding the answer you already knew.

But there's an even more subtle version of this deflection. It doesn't compare YouTube to worse things. It denies there's a problem at all. It says you're not addicted — you just like staying informed. You're just a curious person. The watching isn't compulsion. It's choice. That's the last major illusion the Big Monster has, and it goes straight to identity. We'll look at it next.
