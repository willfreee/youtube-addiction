# Chapter 15 — When the Little Monster Cries Out

I know you might still be stirring right now. Even after everything in the last chapter — even after the whole ledger was read out, every claim checked and found empty — there may still be some small movement in you, some faint pull toward the phone, some restlessness that hasn't quite gotten the memo. If that's true, this chapter is for you to read alongside that feeling, not instead of it.

Because here's the thing worth saying plainly, before anything else: that's expected. It doesn't mean the understanding didn't land. It doesn't mean Chapter 14 was wrong, or that some genuine value was hiding in there after all, dodging every test until now. It just means you're dealing with a conditioned response, and conditioned responses were never going to disappear the exact instant their object was understood to be worthless. They run down. They don't switch off.

Think back to the animal at the back step, the one that learned, over months of reliable feeding, to show up right on schedule every evening, pacing a little, waiting. If the feeding stopped tomorrow — no scraps, no exceptions, nothing — that animal wouldn't simply stop coming the very first night. It would show up anyway, out of habit, pace for a while at an empty step, and eventually wander off. It might do that again the next evening. Maybe even the one after that. Not because the food is secretly still there and it knows something you don't. Because a pattern worn in that deeply doesn't erase itself the moment its reason for existing does. It just runs on fumes for a little while, and then it doesn't.

This matters because there's a very particular trap waiting for you right here, and it's worth naming clearly so you can recognize it the moment it shows up: *if this were really true — if there was really nothing of value in this — then why do I still feel like reaching for it?* That thought sounds like evidence. It feels, in the moment, like a fair challenge to everything you've just worked through. It isn't. It's simply the animal pacing at the step, and mistaking its pacing for proof that the step still has food on it is exactly the mistake this chapter exists to help you avoid.

Here's the distinction worth holding onto, because two very different things can feel almost identical in the body. There's craving — the Little Monster's mechanical residue, a habit still running down, with no real content behind it, no genuine loss attached to it. And there's deprivation — the actual discomfort of losing something valuable, something that mattered. They can produce a similar physical sensation: a restlessness, a pull, a low hum of wanting. But Chapter 14 already settled which one this is. There was nothing of value found in that ledger. So whatever stirs now isn't deprivation. It's just the last of the craving, working its way out.

And it will work its way out — that's the part worth being genuinely reassured by, not just told. This isn't indefinite. It isn't something you need to white-knuckle through for months, bracing every evening the way you might have with a moderation rule that demanded daily upkeep forever. Think of a fire that's stopped being fed. It doesn't go out the second the last log stops getting added. It burns down through its remaining embers first — real heat, for a little while, entirely genuine — and then, on their own, without anyone needing to smother them, those embers finish and go quiet. That's what's left here. Not a fire that needs fighting. Embers that need nothing from you at all except the patience to let them finish.

A few days from now, or maybe sooner, you'll likely notice one of these embers directly. Picture it: you're waiting somewhere — in a line, at a red light, in that specific quiet gap after dinner when the evening hasn't quite decided what it's doing yet — and your hand moves toward your phone almost on its own, the way it's moved a thousand times before at exactly this kind of moment. And then, mid-motion, you catch it. You recognize exactly what's happening, immediately, without needing to think it through. *There it is,* you might think. *Pacing at the step again.* And that recognition alone tends to be enough. There's no alarm in it, no need to fight your hand back down like you're wrestling something. You just notice, maybe almost smile at it, and the moment passes.

That's the encounter worth having with each of these flickers as they come — not resistance, not fear, but something closer to mild amusement. You already know exactly what this small animal is and exactly why it's there. You've met it properly, back in Chapter 3, and you understand its whole mechanism now: not a character flaw, not a verdict on your discipline, just a habit that learned your schedule and hasn't quite finished unlearning it yet. Watching it pace a few more times at a step that's genuinely empty now isn't suffering. It's almost tender, in a strange way — a small, familiar creature, running down the last of something it doesn't know yet has ended.

This is a slightly different kind of noticing than the one Chapter 6 asked of you. That chapter was about correctly identifying, after the fact, what a feeling had actually been evidence of — relief, not rest. This chapter is about anticipating a feeling in advance, so that when it does show up, you're not caught off guard by it, and you don't mistake its arrival for a verdict on anything you've understood.

There is nothing here to brace for. Nothing to fight, nothing that requires effort, no daily vigilance standing at a door the way moderation once demanded. Just a few small, predictable, entirely harmless moments, arriving occasionally for a little while, and passing on their own if you simply let them.

The Little Monster's last few cries were never a verdict on what you've understood — they were just an old, small animal, pacing a step that will never be fed again, until one evening, without ceremony, it simply stops coming.

And on that evening, and the ones after it, you'll likely notice something else entirely — not an absence, not something missing from how the hours feel, but a particular, ordinary quality to time that hasn't been organized around any of this in quite a while.
