9: Someone Else's Life

You've watched someone cook a beautiful meal and felt, for a moment, like you'd cooked it. You've watched someone travel to a place you'll probably never go and felt like you'd been there. You've watched someone build a business, renovate a house, learn a skill, transform their body — and felt a small, warm sense of accomplishment, as if some of what they did had rubbed off on you.

This feeling is not imaginary. It's real. Your brain responds to observed experiences in ways that partially mirror the responses of the person actually having them. When you watch someone cook, the sensory and motor regions of your brain show activity similar to — though not identical to — the activity of the person doing the cooking. When you watch someone walk through a beautiful landscape, your brain processes the visual experience in ways that overlap with how it would process the experience if you were there. This is a genuine human capacity. It's part of how we learn, how we empathize, how we connect.

But here's the question that matters: while you were watching them live their life, what was happening to yours?

Because the warm feeling fades. The video ends. The creator's life continues — they cook another meal, take another trip, build another project. Their skills improve. Their story advances. And you? You're still on the couch. The feeling of having almost been there is gone, replaced by the next video, and the next, and the feeling of having almost been somewhere else.

The Big Monster has a name for this. It calls it inspiration. It calls it entertainment. It calls it harmless. And in one sense, it's right. There is nothing wrong with watching someone cook a meal you'll never cook, or visit a place you'll never visit, or live a life you'll never live. If you did it once, deliberately, and then closed the app and went back to your own life, there would be no problem. That would just be someone watching a video they wanted to watch.

But that's not what's happening, is it? It's not once. It's not deliberate. It's hours, accumulated over days and weeks and years, watching other people do the things you wish you were doing, other people become the person you wish you were becoming. And the watching has started to feel like a substitute for the doing. The feeling of having almost been there has started to feel like enough.

## The Meal You Didn't Cook

Let's be precise about what's happening when you watch someone else's experience. It's not nothing. The feeling is real.

When you watch a cooking video, you really do get a small, diluted version of the sensory experience. You see the ingredients. You hear the sizzle. You watch the hands move. Your brain generates a faint internal simulation of what it would be like to do those movements yourself. At the end, you see the finished dish, and for a moment you feel something — not the full pride of having cooked it, but a small warmth, an echo of satisfaction.

When you watch a travel vlog, you really do see the landscape. You hear the ambient sounds. You follow the creator's journey through a place you've never been. Your brain processes the visual novelty, the beauty, the sense of discovery. For the duration of the video, part of you is there. At the end, you feel something — a small sense of having traveled, a quieting of the restlessness that wants to be somewhere else.

This is a real phenomenon. It's not a trick of the light. But it's also not what it feels like. And the gap between what it feels like and what it actually is — that's where the Parasite lives.

If you ate a photograph of a meal, the experience of looking at it would be real. The colors, the textures, the composition — all real. The hunger it temporarily quiets would be real. But what actually entered your body? Nothing. No nourishment. No sustenance. The photograph gave you the feeling of eating without the substance of food. And an hour later, you'd be hungry again — hungrier, probably, because you'd spent an hour looking at food instead of eating it.

This is the exchange the Parasite brokers. It takes your genuine hunger — for experience, for mastery, for adventure, for a life that feels like yours — and feeds it a photograph. The feeling is real. The hunger quiets for a while. But nothing actually entered you. The hunger is still there, waiting, a little stronger than before.

## The Hours You Didn't Spend

Now let's look at the cost. Not in the abstract. In the specific.

Think back over the past year of watching. You've seen creators cook hundreds of meals. You've watched them travel to dozens of countries. You've followed their fitness journeys, their creative projects, their home renovations, their skill development, their personal transformations. You've been there — in the audience — while they did all of it.

Now ask yourself: in that same year, what did you do?

Not what did you watch. What did you actually do? What skills did you build? What places did you go? What projects did you complete? What meals did you cook that you were proud of? What physical changes did you make? What creative work did you produce? What adventures did you have that were yours — not mediated through someone else's camera, not borrowed from someone else's life, but yours?

The gap between those two lists is the cost. Not time wasted in some abstract sense. Life not lived. Hours that were exchanged for recordings of other people's hours. The creator's life advanced. Their skills grew. Their story accumulated chapters. Yours paused. Not dramatically — just quietly, one video at a time, while the Big Monster whispered that this was fine, that this was relaxation, that you were just unwinding, that you'd get to your own life tomorrow.

The Big Monster doesn't want you to do this accounting. It wants you to keep the cost vague. *It's just entertainment. It doesn't cost me anything.* But time is the one resource you can't get back. Every hour spent watching someone else build is an hour not spent building. Every hour spent watching someone else explore is an hour not spent exploring. The cost isn't just the time — it's the accumulated absence of your own development. The skills you didn't build. The places you didn't go. The person you didn't become, because you were busy watching someone else become someone.

## The Hunger That's Actually Real

But let's be fair to the watching. Let's not pretend it's arbitrary. You don't watch cooking videos because you're indifferent to cooking. You watch them because you genuinely want to cook beautifully. You don't watch travel vlogs because you have no interest in travel. You watch them because you genuinely long to see the world. You don't watch fitness transformations because you don't care about your body. You watch them because you genuinely want to be stronger, healthier, more capable.

The interest is real. The longing is real. This is not the Parasite manufacturing a craving. This is a Real Hunger — one of the genuine, satisfiable human needs we talked about in Chapter 7. You want adventure. You want mastery. You want creativity. You want connection. You want to do things that matter, make things that last, become someone you're proud of. These are not weaknesses. These are what it means to be alive and wanting more from life than mere survival.

The Parasite doesn't create these hungers. It exploits them.

It knows you want to cook. So it serves you cooking videos — thousands of them, an endless supply, each one giving you the feeling of cooking without requiring you to chop an onion or wash a pan. It knows you want to travel. So it serves you travel content that gives you the feeling of having been somewhere without requiring you to buy a ticket, learn a language, step out your front door. It knows you want to transform. So it serves you transformation stories that give you the feeling of having changed without requiring you to break a sweat or form a habit or do the slow, unglamorous work of actually becoming different.

And the real hunger — the genuine, human need for a life that feels like yours — doesn't get fed. It gets numbed. The cooking videos mute the hunger to cook, just enough that you don't feel the urgency to actually do it. The travel vlogs quiet the restlessness, just enough that you don't feel the discomfort that might push you to plan a trip. The transformation content satisfies the yearning, just enough that you don't feel the pressure to change.

The hunger is still there. It's been there the whole time. It's grown, actually — because hungers that are fed with substitutes don't shrink. They deepen. You want to cook more than you did a year ago, but you've cooked less. You want to travel more, but you've gone nowhere. You want to change more desperately, but the gap between who you are and who you want to be has widened, not narrowed. The Parasite has been feeding you sawdust while the real hunger grew.

## The Inspiration That Stays on the Screen

At this point, you might be thinking of exceptions. The video that actually inspired you to try something. The recipe you made because a creator walked you through it. The workout you started because someone's transformation convinced you it was possible. The place you visited because a vlog showed you it existed.

These moments are real. They happen. And the Big Monster loves them — because they provide cover for everything else. *See? Watching isn't passive. It leads to action. It's productive.*

Let's look at those moments honestly. Out of the last hundred videos you watched about things you'd like to do, how many led to action? Not to the feeling of being inspired. Not to the thought *I should try that someday.* To actual action — something you did, something you made, something you changed. What's the ratio? One in twenty? One in fifty? One in a hundred?

And what about the other ninety-nine? What did they lead to? They led to the next video. They gave you the feeling of inspiration — the warm, motivated, slightly elevated sensation of having encountered something meaningful — and then that feeling faded by the time the next video started. The inspiration stayed on the screen. It never made it into your hands.

This is the distinction that matters. Inspiration is real. It's valuable. It's one of the ways human beings transmit motivation and possibility to each other. A single video that leads to action is worth more than a thousand that don't. But the Parasite has learned to serve inspiration-flavored content — content that gives you the feeling of being inspired without ever tipping into the harder thing. The feeling of inspiration becomes the product. And the product is consumed. And the consumption replaces the action the inspiration was supposed to fuel.

You are capable of being inspired into action. The few videos that actually changed your behavior prove that. The tragedy is that you usually stop at the feeling. The Parasite has trained you to confuse the warmth of inspiration with the substance of change. You finish the video feeling motivated, and you credit the video for motivating you, and you move on to the next video — without ever noticing that the motivation didn't survive the transition.

## The Energy That Got Siphoned Off

There's something else happening here, something harder to see but more important than the ratio of watching to doing.

The desire to travel, to create, to build, to change — that desire has a function. It's not just a feeling. It's a motivational force. It's supposed to be uncomfortable. The restlessness you feel when you want to go somewhere but haven't gone — that restlessness has a job. It's supposed to push you toward planning, saving, booking, going. The dissatisfaction you feel when you want to create something but haven't created it — that dissatisfaction has a job. It's supposed to push you toward the blank page, the empty canvas, the first ugly draft.

Discomfort is fuel. Yearning is energy. The gap between who you are and who you want to be is supposed to generate pressure — and that pressure is supposed to produce motion.

What happens when you relieve that pressure with a video?

The restlessness quiets. The dissatisfaction fades. The gap feels smaller. You've just watched someone travel beautifully, and for a moment, the part of you that wanted to travel feels less urgent. You've just watched someone create something wonderful, and for a moment, the part of you that wanted to create feels less restless. The pressure has been released — not by addressing its source, but by numbing its signal.

The Parasite doesn't just steal your time. It steals the discomfort that might have changed your life.

This is the deepest layer of the vicarious illusion. You're not confusing watching with doing — you know the difference. You don't literally think you've been to Morocco because you watched a travel vlog. The illusion isn't cognitive. It's emotional. It operates at the level of satisfaction, not belief. The part of you that needed to go somewhere, do something, become someone — that part got fed a video, and it stopped pushing. The pressure dropped. The hunger quieted. And you stayed where you were.

If you're not confusing watching with doing, why does the hunger to do the thing feel less urgent after you've watched someone else do it? What exactly just got satisfied — and was it supposed to be satisfied by a screen?

The energy that might have become a plane ticket became a watch history entry instead. The pressure that might have become a first draft became a playlist. The dissatisfaction that might have become a change became a session. Over and over, week after week, year after year. The Parasite siphoning off the fuel before the engine can start.

## The Main Character of Your Life

There's one more question to ask, and it's the one the Big Monster works hardest to keep you from considering.

When you're eighty years old, looking back at your life, who do you want the main character of that life to have been?

Not the supporting characters. Not the people you admired from a distance. Not the creators whose journeys you followed, whose milestones you celebrated, whose stories you can recount in detail because you were there for every upload. Who do you want the protagonist to have been?

Because right now, the protagonist is them. The creators are the ones doing things. They're the ones whose stories advance, whose skills deepen, whose lives accumulate chapters. You're in the audience. You're watching. You know their narratives better than you know your own — because their narratives are edited, packaged, and delivered to you in twenty-minute installments, while your own narrative is diffuse, unedited, and easy to ignore.

The Parasite's final victory is convincing you that this is a fair trade. That being a spectator is almost as good as being a protagonist. That watching someone else's story is a valid substitute for living your own. The Big Monster wraps this in flattering language: *You're a curious person. You appreciate quality content. You're part of a community.* And all of that might be true. But none of it changes the basic fact: while you've been watching, your own story has been on pause.

The creators you watch are not going to live your life for you. They can't. They're busy living theirs. Every hour you spend in their world is an hour you didn't spend in yours. Every milestone of theirs you celebrate is a milestone you didn't reach. Every adventure of theirs you experience vicariously is an adventure you didn't have.

This isn't an argument for guilt. Guilt is just another form of paralysis, and paralysis is what the Parasite wants. This is an argument for clarity. The cost of vicarious living isn't measured in wasted time — it's measured in unlived life. And the life that's going unlived is yours.

## The Thing That's Still Waiting

I want to end this chapter somewhere different than you might expect. Not on a warning. On a permission.

The things you love watching — the cooking, the travel, the building, the creating, the transforming — those interests are not random. They're not weaknesses. They're not evidence that you're a passive person who'd rather watch than do. They're evidence of a real hunger. A genuine desire. A life that wants to be larger than it currently is.

That hunger hasn't gone anywhere. It's been numbed, but it's still there. It's been fed substitutes, but it's still hungry for the real thing. The fact that you've been watching someone else do it doesn't mean you can't do it. It means you haven't yet — but "not yet" is not the same as "never."

Every creator you admire was once someone who had never done the thing. They started. Badly. Probably without an audience. Probably without knowing what they were doing. The only difference between you and them isn't talent, or resources, or luck. It's that they did the thing while you watched them do it.

What would happen if you did the thing? Not for an audience. Not for a video. Not to be good at it. Just to do it — to cook the meal, take the trip, start the project, make the thing, move your body, write the words, build the skill. Not to become a creator. To become a person who does things instead of watching them.

You don't need to be good at it. You don't need to film it. You don't need anyone to watch. You just need to stop trading your own finite, unrepeatable life for recordings of someone else's. The real thing is still available. It's been waiting for you the whole time, on the other side of the screen.

Every hour you spend watching someone else live is an hour of your own life you didn't live. Not an hour of relaxation, not an hour of harmless entertainment — an hour of the only life you have, exchanged for a recording of someone else's. The creators you watch are building their lives. Their skills grow. Their stories advance. Yours pauses. The vicarious feeling — the warm sense of almost having been there — is the Parasite's most personal theft: it takes your genuine hunger for adventure, mastery, and meaning, and feeds it a simulation that leaves the hunger intact and the life unlived.

You don't need to be a creator. You don't need to be good at anything. You just need to be the main character of your own story, not a spectator in someone else's.

But being the main character means being present for what's happening — not just in your own life, but in the world. And that raises a fear the Big Monster knows how to exploit. What if you miss something? What if everyone else is seeing something you're not? What if you fall behind, lose touch, become irrelevant? That fear has a name, and it's one of the Parasite's most effective tools.
