# Who's Really Choosing?

Think about the last time you opened YouTube without a specific video in mind.

You tapped the app, and there they were — thumbnails, recommendations, a feed already populated with things you might want to watch. You hadn't searched yet. You hadn't expressed a preference. You hadn't done anything except open the app. But the platform already knew what to show you. And what it showed you determined what you watched.

So who, exactly, made the first choice?

This is not a small question. It cuts to the center of one of the Big Monster's most carefully protected beliefs: that you are the one in control. That you decide what to watch, when to watch, how long to watch. That the platform is a neutral library and you are a free agent wandering its aisles, following your curiosity wherever it leads. That the hours you spend on YouTube are hours you chose to spend.

The Algorithm Illusion is the machinery behind that belief. And once you see how it works — really see it, not just understand it abstractly — the feeling of free choice starts to look less like freedom and more like a tour route someone else designed.

The Menu That Was Written Before You Sat Down

Let's start with the moment you open the app. No search. No specific intention. Just the homepage.

What you see is not a random sample of YouTube's billions of videos. It's not even a broad reflection of "what's popular" or "what's new." It's a highly personalized, algorithmically curated selection of content that has been chosen specifically for you — or more precisely, chosen to maximize the probability that you will click on something and keep watching.

The algorithm has been building a model of you for years. It knows what you click on. It knows what you watch all the way through and what you abandon after thirty seconds. It knows what time of day you watch, what day of the week, what device you're on, what you watched last time, what you watched three months ago, what kinds of thumbnails make you pause, what titles make you curious, what topics you've been exploring lately and what topics you've drifted away from. It knows your preferences better than you do — because it has data you don't have access to, and it's been optimizing against that data every single day.

By the time the homepage loads on your screen, the algorithm has already made thousands of decisions about what to show you. It has excluded millions of videos it knows you won't click on. It has elevated the ones it predicts you will. The set of options you're choosing from was curated before you arrived.

Now, imagine someone handed you a menu with only five dishes on it — all of which they knew, from years of tracking your eating habits, you were highly likely to order. And they said to you, "Choose whatever you want." Are you freely choosing your meal? Or are you choosing within a set of options that was already chosen for you?

You do have a choice. You're not being force-fed. You can pick from the five dishes. But you can't pick something that isn't on the menu. And the menu was designed by someone whose only goal is to keep you at the table.

This is the first layer of the Algorithm Illusion. Choice on YouTube doesn't begin with your click. It begins with the algorithm's curation. The platform doesn't show you "everything." It shows you a tiny, optimized subset designed to trigger engagement. Your click — the moment that feels like agency — is actually the final step in a chain of decisions the algorithm already made. You're choosing, yes. But you're choosing from options that were chosen for you.

The Gap That Disappeared

Now let's look at what happens after you click.

You're watching a video. It's fine — maybe good, maybe just interesting enough to keep going. It ends. And before you can decide whether you want to continue watching anything at all, the next video begins.

You know this moment. The countdown in the corner — five seconds, three, one. Maybe you barely register it anymore. The next video just starts. The title appears. The thumbnail fades. The content plays. And you keep watching.

What just happened? You didn't choose to watch that next video. You didn't even choose to keep watching in general. The platform removed the moment where that choice would have been made. There used to be a gap — a space between videos where you had to actively decide to continue. That gap was a natural stopping point. It was the place where your brain could ask itself, Do I want to keep going? Am I still enjoying this? Should I do something else now?

Autoplay eliminated that gap. Not because users demanded it. Because the platform discovered, through relentless testing, that removing the gap increased watch time. When continuing is automatic, continuing is what happens. When stopping requires an active decision, stopping happens far less often — because the human brain tends toward the path of least resistance, and "continue" is now the path of zero resistance.

Think about what this means for the idea that you're choosing to keep watching. If I design a room so that the door only opens outward and requires you to push a heavy bar, but the chair you're sitting in is comfortable and the screen in front of you keeps changing to new, mildly interesting things — are you choosing to stay in the room? Or are you simply failing to overcome the friction I built into leaving?

You're not choosing to keep watching. You're failing to choose to stop. And the difference is everything. The Big Monster wants you to believe that staying is an affirmative choice — evidence that you're engaged, interested, in control. But staying is just the default. It's what happens when you don't intervene. And the platform has been engineered, layer by layer, to make intervention as difficult as possible while making passivity as seamless as possible.

This is the second layer of the Algorithm Illusion. Your "choice" to continue isn't a choice at all. It's the absence of a choice to stop — and the choice to stop has been deliberately made harder than the choice to continue.

The Machine That Plays Itself

Nowhere is this more visible than in Shorts.

You open the Shorts feed. A video plays immediately. You didn't click on it. You didn't even see a thumbnail or read a title. It just started. The content is already happening, with or without your consent.

You watch for a few seconds. Maybe it grabs you. Maybe it doesn't. If it doesn't, you swipe. The next video plays immediately. Swipe, start. Swipe, start. The "choice" has been reduced to a single binary: stay on this video or swipe to the next one. And "stay" is the default — the video is already playing. You have to actively reject it to move on. But moving on doesn't mean stopping. It means more content. There's no "end" to the Shorts feed. No final video. No point where the platform says, You've seen everything. You're done. The supply is infinite. The feed never ends.

What kind of choice is happening here? You can swipe past things you don't like. That feels like agency. But the machine is still playing. The content is still flowing. The only real choice — the choice to stop entirely — is the one choice the interface makes hardest. There's no "stop" button. No natural endpoint. You have to break out of the loop manually, by closing the app or navigating away, against the current of a design that wants you to keep swiping forever.

Imagine someone designed a slot machine that started spinning the moment you looked at it. You didn't pull a lever. You didn't insert a coin. It just started spinning. And it kept spinning, reel after reel, showing you results — some interesting, some not. You could pull a lever to skip a result you didn't like. But there was no lever to stop the machine entirely. To stop, you had to walk away from the machine — and the machine was designed to make walking away feel like missing something.

Would you say you were choosing to keep gambling? Or would you say the machine was designed to make stopping harder than continuing?

Shorts is that machine. It eliminates choice almost entirely. The content plays itself. The feed never ends. Your only "choice" is when to break out of the loop — and breaking out requires more effort than staying in. The platform has stacked the deck so that continuing is effortless and leaving is effortful. That's not freedom. That's a trap that mimics freedom.

The Tour Guide Who Chooses Your Destination

So far we've talked about the mechanics of choice — how the platform removes stopping points, makes continuing the default, and reduces agency to a binary swipe. But there's a deeper layer to the Algorithm Illusion, and it's the one that most effectively disguises itself as freedom.

You believe the algorithm follows your curiosity. You search for something you're interested in. You click on a video that appeals to you. The algorithm notes your interest and recommends more like it. You feel seen. You feel understood. The algorithm gets me, you think. It shows me things I actually want to watch.

But the algorithm doesn't just follow your curiosity. It shapes it.

Here's how it works. You click on a video — maybe something slightly outside your usual interests, something the algorithm recommended on the homepage or in the sidebar. The video is engaging. You watch it. The algorithm registers the engagement and adjusts its model of you. Now it recommends more content in that vein. You click on one of those recommendations. The engagement deepens. The algorithm adjusts again. Over weeks and months, your "interests" shift — not because your intrinsic curiosity changed direction, but because the algorithm fed you a path of content that gradually led you somewhere new.

You followed the path. You clicked each time. Each individual step felt like curiosity. But the destination — the place you ended up, the content you now watch regularly — was not something you chose. It was something the algorithm discovered you would engage with, and then led you toward, one video at a time.

If a tour guide takes you through a city and you enjoy the walk — the sights are interesting, the route is pleasant, you're never bored — are you exploring the city? Or are you following a route someone else designed to keep you walking past the shops that pay them commission?

You're not exploring. You're being guided. And the guide's priority isn't your curiosity. It's your attention. The algorithm doesn't care whether you're genuinely interested in the content or merely engaged by it. It only cares that you keep watching. And it has learned, across billions of data points, exactly how to keep you watching — by serving content that triggers just enough curiosity, just enough emotion, just enough surprise to prevent you from closing the app.

What feels like "I'm interested in this" is often "the algorithm predicted I would engage with this and served it to me at the right moment." The interest is genuine in the moment — you really are curious about the video you're watching. But the curiosity was elicited. It was sparked by a thumbnail, a title, a recommendation algorithm that knew exactly what would make you click. You didn't wake up curious about that topic. The algorithm woke up your curiosity, and then took credit for satisfying it.

A Hundred Steps Off the Cliff

There's one more piece to the Algorithm Illusion, and it's the one that makes the whole thing feel invisible while it's happening.

It's late. You've been watching for a while — longer than you intended, probably. You think, One more video and then I'll stop. The video plays. It ends. Autoplay serves the next one — an interesting thumbnail, a compelling title. You think, Okay, that one looks good too. Just one more. And then another. And another.

Each individual decision feels small. Reasonable. Freely made. You could stop after this one. You will stop after this one. But the next one also looks small and reasonable and freely choosable. And so you keep going.

The hour that disappears doesn't feel like a single decision. It feels like a series of tiny, inconsequential choices, none of which seemed like the moment where things got out of control. But that's exactly the design. The platform breaks the choice to continue into such small increments — one more video, one more scroll, one more swipe — that no single increment feels like the moment where agency is lost.

If each individual step off a cliff is only six inches, and you take a hundred of them, are you choosing to fall? Or are you being led somewhere by an architecture that made each step feel too small to resist?

Agency isn't lost in one big decision. It's lost in a hundred small ones, each made easier by the design of the platform. The Algorithm Illusion's final move is to make the loss of choice feel like a series of choices. You feel in control the whole time. You are in control, in the narrow sense that you could, theoretically, stop at any moment. But the moments are designed to keep coming, and each one is designed to be slightly harder to refuse than the last.

This is the Magic Trick again, in a new form. The magician doesn't make you stay. He just makes leaving slightly harder than continuing, and then steps back while you do the rest. The "choice" is the misdirection.

What the Big Monster Needs You to Believe

Why does any of this matter? You're still watching. You're still reading. The instruction from Chapter 1 hasn't changed. You don't need to do anything differently.

It matters because the Big Monster's entire structure depends on one core belief: that you are freely choosing to watch. If watching is freely chosen, it must be valuable. If you keep choosing it, it must be giving you something. The hours you spend are hours you wanted to spend. The videos you watch are videos you wanted to watch. You are an autonomous agent, acting on your own preferences, and YouTube simply provides what you ask for.

This is the story the Big Monster tells. It's a flattering story. It makes you feel like an explorer, a learner, a person with agency and taste. And it's a story that the Algorithm Illusion is designed to support.

But if that story collapses — if you see that your "choices" are largely responses to cues engineered by a machine designed to keep you inside it — then what does the Big Monster have left? If you're not freely choosing, then the watching isn't evidence of value. If the watching isn't evidence of value, then the beliefs that keep you trapped — it relaxes me, it comforts me, it informs me — lose their foundation. They become visible as what they are: stories the Big Monster told you, reinforced by a platform that benefits from you believing them.

The Big Monster needs you to believe you're in control. The algorithm needs you to feel like you're choosing. The entire system depends on the illusion of agency.

And you can break that illusion without changing a single behavior. You don't need to stop watching. You don't need to resist the algorithm. You just need to see it. Every time you open the app, notice the homepage that was populated before you arrived. Every time a video ends, notice the autoplay countdown removing the gap. Every time you open Shorts, notice that the content starts without you. Every time you think just one more, notice the architecture that makes "one more" feel small and stopping feel large.

You are not freely choosing what to watch on YouTube. You are navigating a highly engineered environment designed to make continuing effortless and stopping effortful. Autoplay removes the moment of decision. The algorithm shapes your preferences, then presents the shaped result as "your interests." Shorts eliminates choice almost entirely, reducing your agency to a single binary — stay or swipe — while making "stay" the default.

None of this means you're a passive victim. It means you've been playing a game whose rules were written to keep you playing. Once you see the rules, the game changes. You can still play. But you can no longer believe you're the one who dealt the cards.

Once you've seen the strings on a puppet, you can't ever watch the show the same way again. The puppet still moves. The show still plays. But you're no longer seeing a character. You're seeing a hand inside a piece of fabric. And the hand doesn't belong to you.

There's another dimension to this, though. It's not just that the algorithm is steering what you watch. It's what you're watching. So much of the content is other people living their lives — creating, traveling, building, achieving. And watching them can start to feel like doing it yourself. That's the next illusion, and it's one of the most personally costly ones the Big Monster has.
