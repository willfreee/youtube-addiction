The Easy Way to Quit YouTube Addiction

Chapter 3 — Meet the Little Monster

Give it a name. That thing that kept reaching for the next video before you'd decided anything — it's time you knew what to call it.

Call it the Little Monster.

Not because it's frightening. It isn't. Not because it's evil, or clever, or has any grand design on your life — it doesn't have the depth for any of that. Call it the Little Monster because that's exactly the right size and shape for what it is: small, persistent, and hungry in a very specific, very narrow way. It wants the next video. That's the whole of its ambition. It doesn't want you to be happy or unhappy, successful or stuck, it doesn't care about your job or your sleep or your relationships — it has exactly one interest, and that interest is the next autoplay, and everything else about your life is simply outside its field of vision entirely.

Here's the first thing you need to know about it, and it matters more than anything else in this chapter: the Little Monster is not you.

I know that might not be how it's felt. When the craving shows up — and you know the feeling I mean, the specific itch in the thumb, the low internal hum of just check one thing rising up out of nowhere while you're standing in a checkout line or half-listening to a conversation that's gone quiet for a second too long — it doesn't announce itself as separate from you. It just feels like wanting. Like you, wanting. And because it's felt that way for so long, you've probably folded it into who you think you are. I'm just someone who gets like this. I've always been the type to get pulled into things. I'm probably just wired for this stuff. You've made it identity when it was never anything of the sort.

So let's take it apart properly, because once you see what it's actually made of, that identity story stops holding together on its own.

Nobody is born craving autoplay. Think about that for one second — really think about it, because it's not a throwaway line, it's the whole foundation of this chapter. There is no version of a newborn human being that arrives into the world with a preference for algorithmically queued video content. That craving did not exist in you at any point by default. It had to be built. Someone — or rather, something, a system — had to install it, and it did that the same way anything gets installed in a person: through repetition, paired with a small reward, over and over, until the pairing became automatic. You watched something, and a little hit of stimulation followed. You watched again, another hit followed. Do that enough times — and it doesn't take many — and the wanting stops needing the reward to show up first. The wanting starts showing up on its own, in advance, expecting to be fed.

Picture, for a second, an animal that's been fed scraps from the same back step every evening around the same time. Nobody trained it on purpose. Nobody sat it down and taught it a lesson. It just kept showing up, and food kept appearing, and after a few weeks it doesn't wait to see if food appears anymore — it shows up right on schedule, expectant, sometimes pacing a little by the door before anyone's even opened it. That animal isn't broken. It isn't badly behaved. It's not making a moral choice when it shows up hungry at six o'clock. It simply learned, through nothing more mysterious than repetition, that this particular time and place reliably produces this particular reward — and now its body expects it, whether or not anyone intends to feed it that night.

That's the Little Monster. That's the whole mechanism, laid bare. It's not a flaw in your character. It's not proof that you're weak-willed, or uniquely susceptible, or somehow different from people who "just don't have this problem." It's an animal that learned to show up at the back step, because for a long time, every time it showed up, something was there.

And here's where the reframe actually does its work, because this is the part that changes everything: if it was built, it can be unbuilt. Not fought. Not overpowered. Unbuilt — the same simple way it was built in the first place, which is to say through the repetition simply not continuing. Nobody trains that animal out of showing up by yelling at it, or by standing at the door gritting their teeth against the urge to feed it while it paces and whines. They just stop putting food on the step. And the animal — not punished, not shamed, just no longer reinforced — eventually stops showing up too, because there was never anything more to it than a pattern learned through repetition, and unlearned the same way.

You are not the animal at the door. That's the distinction worth holding onto, maybe the most important sentence in this whole chapter: you are the one who owns the house. The Little Monster is a passenger in your life, not the driver, no matter how loudly it sometimes seems to be steering — it's loud precisely because it's small and single-minded and has nothing else to think about except the next video, while you have an entire life to think about besides. Its hunger has never once had anything to do with your worth, your discipline, or who you fundamentally are. It's a habit that learned your schedule. That's all it ever was.

The Little Monster isn't who you are. It's just a small, hungry habit that learned your name — and habits, unlike character, can always be starved out.

But there's still a question sitting underneath all of this that we haven't touched yet, and it's worth naming honestly before we go any further: if the craving itself is this small, this mechanical, this unremarkable once you actually look at it — then why does the thought of not feeding it still feel like it would cost you something? Why does some part of you still believe you genuinely need what it's asking for?

That belief isn't coming from the Little Monster at all. It's coming from somewhere else entirely.
